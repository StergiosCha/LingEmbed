Theories Under Pressure

Embedding Linguistic Frameworks in Neural Models

What happens when a linguistic framework is built into a neural model as a working component? And can rival theories be compared this way?

about · 01

One question, two communities

Neural language models are fluent but theory-blind. They make no explicit commitment to any account of syntax, semantics or pragmatics, and it is hard to tell whether a given linguistic structure does any work inside them. Decades of linguistic theory make exactly this structure explicit: Categorial Grammar, HPSG, Minimalism, Construction Grammar, Dynamic Syntax and type-theoretic semantics.

LingEmbed treats a linguistic theory as a callable, parameterised, differentiable module that competes under data pressure. No existing workshop puts this question first.

For NLP

Can structure derived from a theory make models more compositional, more sample-efficient, easier to verify, or easier to interpret?

For theoretical linguistics

When two frameworks compete in the same network, which one earns its keep, and on which phenomena? This would give theory a large-scale empirical test beyond introspective judgements.

Pick a framework. The analysis of every student reads a book is drawn on the left. In the middle, its nodes are placed so that ‖hᵢ − hⱼ‖² = dtree(i, j), which is the geometry a structural probe looks for. On the right, the result is passed through a small neural encoder. Hover a node to follow it through all three stages. This is an illustration of the question, not a proposed method.

tracks · 03

Four tracks

track 1

Grammars and theories as modules

Full or partial implementations of the frameworks above as differentiable, callable components, and schemes for compiling a theory into tensors.

track 2

Probing for theory

Tests of whether pretrained models encode the structure a theory predicts (constituency, dependency, type structure, scope), and of how that structure is spread across the representation.

track 3

Theory competition and evaluation

Rival frameworks placed in the same network and measured on which helps where. This includes benchmarks and cognitive tests of whether an embedded theory produces human-like processing, not only a task gain.

track 4

Enabling resources and negative results

Treebanks, meaning representations, formalisms, and honest reports of what does not work yet.

scope · 04

A laddered call: four ways in

A call that asked only for complete framework implementations would draw very few papers, because few groups in any framework can compile a broad-coverage grammar into a differentiable module. Embedding a whole grammar is therefore not an entry requirement. Theoretical linguists can take part without a GPU, and ML researchers can take part without committing to a framework. All four rungs are reviewed as full contributions.

4

Complete implementations

A framework compiled as a whole into a differentiable component. This is what the workshop exists to make more common.

3

Partial or phenomenon-scoped implementations

One construction, one phenomenon, or one fragment of a grammar, built into a model.

2

Theory-committed probing

Nothing is compiled. A named theory's predictions are tested against what a pretrained model encodes.

1

Enabling resources and negative results

Where most of the community already works, and where a first edition can expect most submissions. Negative results are explicitly welcome.

format · 05

Papers, plus a shared-task design forum

The workshop has two outputs: a paper programme, and the design of a shared task, built together with the community rather than imposed in advance. There is no shared task in the first edition. The natural task ("compile your framework and compete") cannot be run yet. Broad-coverage implementations are few. A two-month prototype cannot be compared fairly with decades of engineering in another framework. Any measured difference would therefore reflect how mature an implementation is, not how adequate the theory is. A leaderboard whose numbers cannot support the claim they appear to make would damage the very comparison this workshop exists to enable.

The closing forum asks

  • What would count as evidence that a theory helps?
  • Which comparisons can be identified, and what must be held fixed across frameworks?
  • Which phenomena already have evaluation data and human processing measures?
  • Which formats are realistic for the community that actually turns up?

Short task-design contributions

To keep the forum concrete, the call invites short task-design contributions alongside regular papers. These can be desiderata, pilot studies, candidate evaluation items, or feasibility analyses of earlier shared tasks. They are reviewed like any other submission and seed the discussion.

Output: a community blueprint, written up as a co-authored report with every contributor credited. It will set out the task's format, target phenomena, data, metrics and division of labour. The second edition's shared task will be built on it.

interactive · 06

Theory-to-tensor compilation, in miniature

One of the oldest ways to put structure into vectors is Smolensky's tensor product representation. Each filler (a word) is bound to a role (its position in the framework selected above) with an outer product, and the bindings are summed: T = Σ fᵢ ⊗ rᵢ. With orthonormal roles, unbinding T·rₖ returns fₖ exactly. Turn orthonormality off to see crosstalk. In Categorial Grammar the roles are tree positions, in Construction Grammar they are construction slots, and in Dynamic Syntax they are tree-node addresses.

    How LingEmbed relates to neighbouring venues

    The organising team spans Europe and East Asia, so hybrid delivery is how it already works.

    Task-design contributions are circulated in advance, so remote participants co-author the blueprint on equal terms.

    Light-touch mentoring for first-time student authors, including theoretical linguists who are new to neural modelling.
